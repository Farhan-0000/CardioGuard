# Introduction

People who live with raised blood pressure make most of their health decisions alone: when to re-check a reading, whether a dizzy spell is related to a new tablet, or whether a number on the home monitor means they should go to hospital. Conversational artificial intelligence (AI) appears to offer the patient, always-available explainer they need, yet the same systems can state an invented drug interaction with confidence, personalise advice to a patient they know almost nothing about, or treat a hypertensive emergency as a routine question. This report describes **MedBridge**, a consultation assistant for hypertension and cardiovascular care built on one principle: *before generating an answer, the system must decide whether it is entitled to give one*. The project is presented under the title *CardioGuard*; MedBridge is the name of the software system throughout.

## Background and Motivation

Hypertension affects about one adult in three worldwide. The first WHO global report on hypertension estimates that the number of people living with it doubled to roughly 1.3 billion between 1990 and 2019 and that around four in five are not adequately treated [@who2023globalreport]. India carries a large share of this burden: the ICMR-INDIAB survey of 113,043 adults estimated a prevalence of 35.5 per cent using JNC-8 criteria [@anjana2023indiab], and the NFHS-5 household survey found that only 67 per cent of women and 54 per cent of men had ever had their blood pressure (BP) measured [@iips2021nfhs5]. Control programmes depend on patients who understand their readings, take their medicines and return for follow-up, which creates a clear need for trustworthy education between visits.

Hypertension guidance is also highly individual and not uniform across sources. The same reading can be routine in one person and urgent in another, and targets and drug choices change with chronic kidney disease (CKD) [@kdigo2021bp], diabetes [@ada2026cvd] or pregnancy, which the WHO pharmacological guideline excludes altogether [@who2021pharm]. The 2024 ESC guideline [@mcevoy2024esc], the 2023 ESH guideline [@mancia2023esh] and the 2025 AHA/ACC guideline [@jones2025aha] use different categories and targets. Dependence on patient context motivates a check of whether enough is known about the patient; divergence between sources motivates answers that cite the specific passage they rely on.

Large language models (LLMs) have meanwhile become a common first stop for health questions. Health professionals preferred chatbot replies to physician replies for 195 forum questions in 78.6 per cent of evaluations, but judged quality and empathy rather than verified correctness [@ayers2023physicianchatbot]. Reviews of LLMs in medicine and biomedicine name inaccuracy, fabricated content, misuse and patient-data privacy as the main concerns [@thirunavukarasu2023llmmedicine; @tian2024chatgptbiomed], and even high-scoring systems such as Med-PaLM 2, a proprietary cloud model with strong examination scores, needed separate testing on adversarial questions [@singhal2025medpalm2]. A privacy-preserving assistant for Indian patients needs a different design: grounded, cautious and able to run on a single consumer laptop.

## Risks of LLM Health Assistants and the Limits of Retrieval

The literature and the earlier reviews of this project identify the failure modes in Table 1.1; Chapter 2 reviews the underlying work. Hallucinations may contradict the source or add content it cannot verify [@ji2023survey; @huang2025survey], and in medicine they are harmful when they could change a clinical decision [@kim2025medhallucination]. They are also common: an automated audit found that 50 to 90 per cent of medical answers from seven LLMs were not fully supported by the sources they cited [@wu2025sourcecheckup]. Grounding reduces but does not remove the problem, since the best of twelve clinical retrieval pipelines still hallucinated in 5.8 per cent of answers [@wolk2025ragcds] and clinical notes generated with the full transcript available contained 1.47 per cent hallucinated sentences [@asgari2025creola]. Swapping brand and generic drug names lowers model accuracy by about 1 to 10 per cent [@gallifant2024rabbits], which matters where patients name medicines by brand. Symptomatic severe hypertension calls for urgent assessment [@nice2019ng136], but a probabilistic generator gives no guarantee of escalation; instructions hidden in untrusted text can steer an LLM application [@greshake2023notwhat; @owasp2025llmtop10]; and sending health conversations to a third-party service raises obligations under India's Digital Personal Data Protection Act, 2023 [@dpdp2023india].

Table: Failure modes of LLM health assistants and MedBridge safeguards
| Failure mode | Illustrative situation | MedBridge safeguard |
|---|---|---|
| Hallucination | A dose or interaction found in no guideline is stated as fact | Evidence Gate before generation; answers restricted to retrieved passages; citation validation |
| Unsafe personalisation | A beta-blocker is suggested without knowing the patient has asthma; a target is given without knowing about CKD or pregnancy | Context Gate (SOFT-ASK) before retrieval, with a loop-breaker after two requests |
| Unanswerable question | A fabricated drug, a false premise or a non-cardiac topic | Evidence Gate routes to ABSTAIN or GENERALIZE; ABSTAIN is a pre-vetted template |
| Missed emergency | "BP is 200/120 and my chest feels tight" is answered with lifestyle advice | Deterministic emergency fast-path before any LLM call; ESCALATE template |
| Brand-name confusion | An Indian brand is not recognised as the generic drug it contains | Brand-to-generic normalisation and lexical (BM25) retrieval |
| Prompt injection | A message tries to override the system instructions | Patient text isolated in XML blocks; gates and templates outside the generator |
| Privacy leakage | Names or ID numbers reach a model, a log or the database | Redaction before any model call; local-only inference; encryption at rest |

Retrieval-augmented generation (RAG) lets a model draw on an external, updatable knowledge source [@lewis2020rag], and MedBridge builds on it. In its standard form, however, RAG retrieves and generates for every query and has no point at which it can decline. There are two distinct reasons for a medical assistant to hold back. The first is *"I do not know enough about you"*: the guideline may contain the answer, but it depends on facts the patient has not given. The second is *"I do not have enough evidence"*: the knowledge base does not support a specific answer, the question rests on a false premise, or the topic lies outside cardiovascular care. The first calls for a focused follow-up question and can be decided before retrieval; the second calls for general information or a refusal and can only be decided after retrieval. Later RAG methods judge the evidence but not the patient context (Chapter 2). MedBridge makes both decisions explicit.

## Problem Statement and Research Questions

LLM-based health assistants, including retrieval-augmented ones, answer almost every query without first establishing whether the patient context suffices to personalise guidance or whether the retrieved evidence suffices to support a specific claim. They therefore hallucinate, personalise unsafely, answer what they cannot support and may miss emergencies, and the most capable of them depend on cloud models that are hard to reconcile with health-data privacy. The problem addressed is:

*To design, implement and rigorously evaluate a locally deployable, evidence-grounded assistant for hypertension and cardiovascular care that decides, before generating any answer, whether it has sufficient patient context and sufficient guideline evidence to answer safely, and that otherwise asks, generalises, abstains or escalates through deterministic and auditable paths.*

The evaluation is organised around four research questions.

1. **RQ1 (effect of the gate).** Does the full Dual Answerability Gate reduce hallucinated responses and unsafe answers to unanswerable questions compared with a direct LLM, vanilla RAG and RAG with prompt-only safety instructions, using the same model, retrieval and benchmark?
2. **RQ2 (contribution of each gate).** What does each gate contribute when only the Context Gate or only the Evidence Gate is active?
3. **RQ3 (extensions).** Do a calibrated Evidence Gate and a post-generation grounding verifier improve on the base design, and at what cost in coverage and latency?
4. **RQ4 (feasibility).** Can the complete system run on consumer hardware with free, locally hosted models and acceptable response times?

## Scope of the Project

**In scope.** Cardiovascular care centred on hypertension: BP measurement and classification, treatment and lifestyle, hypertensive emergencies, heart failure, atrial fibrillation, coronary disease, dyslipidaemia and risk, BP in CKD, diabetes and pregnancy, and prevention. The output is informational, plain-language guidance with inline citations, together with clarifying questions, general information, safe refusals and emergency referrals, for adults seeking to understand their readings and medicines and for clinicians or reviewers who inspect how a response was produced. Knowledge comes from a curated set of publicly readable guidelines, scientific statements and reviews, and patient-information pages ingested from a manifest, with no open web search. Patient facts are extracted from the conversation and stored as typed events. All language-model inference runs locally through Ollama, and the embedding, reranking and NLI models run locally in the application process; the system was developed on Apple Silicon for a target Windows laptop with a 6 GB GPU. The language is English, including casual and Indian-English phrasing, with common Hinglish red-flag expressions in the emergency classifier. All data are synthetic.

**Out of scope.** Diagnosis, prescription or any change of medicine or dose (the generator is instructed never to advise one); replacement of clinical or emergency triage; non-cardiovascular topics, which are declined; integration with health records or devices; training or fine-tuning of foundation models; clinical validation, regulatory approval and multi-node deployment; and real patient data.

## Expected Outcomes and Organisation of the Report

The expected outcomes are: a working prototype in which every message is routed to exactly one of five actions (ANSWER, SOFT-ASK, GENERALIZE, ABSTAIN, ESCALATE) with every decision explained and audited; an empirical answer to RQ1 to RQ3 from a paired, statistically tested ablation, reported whatever its direction; measured evidence for the components (retrieval, emergency classifier, redaction, routing, robustness to drug names and injection, load and latency) for RQ4; reusable artefacts, namely the HalluBench dataset, the evaluation harness with two judges and a human annotation sheet, and the corpus manifest; a deployment that runs on one laptop with documentation for Windows and macOS; and a research paper prepared for submission.

**Chapter 2** reviews the literature and patents on grounded generation, hallucination, abstention, medical LLMs, evaluation methods, retrieval, security and privacy, and states the research gap. **Chapter 3** describes the method and system design: the architecture, modules, decision algorithms, data model, privacy layer, experimental design and tools. **Chapter 4** presents the implementation, testing and the component and study results, with the limitations. **Chapter 5** concludes and outlines future work. References and appendices with additional results, screenshots, test cases, datasets, publication proof and the contribution statement follow.
