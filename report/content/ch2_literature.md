# Literature and Patent Review

This chapter reviews the work on which MedBridge builds and against which it must be judged: the clinical guidance that defines a correct answer, grounded generation and its failures, abstention and self-correction, medical language models and benchmarks, methods for evaluating hallucination, retrieval components, security and privacy, the literature cited at Review 1 and related patents. It closes with a comparison of existing approaches and the research gap.

## Review Method

The review was organised around the questions raised by the design: which clinical sources define safe guidance, how grounded generation fails, how a system can decide not to answer, how medical LLMs have been built and evaluated, how hallucination can be measured reliably, which retrieval components suit a small local deployment, and which security and privacy obligations apply. Peer-reviewed papers, official guidelines and primary legal or standards texts were preferred; foundational papers were kept for retrieval and statistics, and LLM work was drawn mainly from 2022 onwards. Preprints are used only where no peer-reviewed version exists and are identified as such. Every record was checked against its publisher, proceedings, registry or patent entry. The Review-1 and Review-3 presentations and the design specification were used to recover earlier literature claims; the six literature findings of the Review-1 presentation are matched to publications in Section 2.9.

## Clinical Background: Hypertension Guidance and Its Variability

A guideline-grounded assistant inherits both the strengths and the inconsistencies of its sources. The 2025 AHA/ACC multisociety guideline is the current United States reference for measurement, classification, treatment thresholds, targets and severe hypertension, and has received several published corrections [@jones2025aha]. The 2017 ACC/AHA guideline remains the classic source for separating a hypertensive emergency, in which severely raised BP (typically above 180/120 mmHg) comes with new or worsening target-organ damage, from urgency without such damage [@whelton2018acc]. European guidance differs: the 2024 ESC guideline adds an elevated-BP category and recommends a treated systolic target of 120 to 129 mmHg where tolerated [@mcevoy2024esc], whereas the 2023 ESH guideline keeps the classic grades [@mancia2023esh]. The 2020 ISH guideline separates "essential" from "optimal" care for different resource settings [@unger2020ish], and the WHO pharmacological guideline covers thresholds, first-line classes and task sharing but excludes pregnancy [@who2021pharm]. NICE NG136 lists criteria for same-day referral, including a clinic BP of 180/120 mmHg or higher with signs such as new confusion, chest pain or heart failure [@nice2019ng136]. Comorbidity changes the answer further: KDIGO recommends a systolic target below 120 mmHg in CKD [@kdigo2021bp], the ADA revises its cardiovascular recommendations yearly [@ada2026cvd], and the heart failure [@heidenreich2022hf], atrial fibrillation [@joglar2024af], chronic coronary disease [@virani2023ccd] and primary prevention [@arnett2019primary] guidelines assume clinician oversight of medicines.

Five design implications follow. Answers must cite the specific source they rely on, because sources differ. Many questions cannot be answered safely without facts such as kidney disease, diabetes or pregnancy, which motivates a context check. Emergency criteria are explicit enough to be encoded as deterministic rules. Clinician-oriented text must be rephrased for patients without losing its grounding. And because guidelines are revised, the knowledge base must record each document's edition.

## Retrieval-Augmented Generation and Hallucination

RAG couples a generator with a dense retriever over an external collection, so that knowledge can be updated without retraining and answers are more factual than those of purely parametric models [@lewis2020rag]. Its limitation for a safety-critical setting is structural: retrieval is always performed and an answer is always produced. MedBridge is a modular RAG system that adds query expansion and drug-name normalisation, fuses two retrievers with reranking, and inserts decision modules before and after retrieval.

Hallucinations may contradict the source (*intrinsic*) or add unverifiable content (*extrinsic*) [@ji2023survey]; for LLMs they divide into *factuality* errors and *faithfulness* errors, with failure modes that persist in retrieval-augmented systems [@huang2025survey]. Retrieval failure and unfaithful generation are therefore separate risks, addressed in MedBridge by the Evidence Gate before generation and by evidence-only generation, citation validation and, in the V2 variant, claim verification afterwards. Kim et al. define a medical hallucination as output that could alter clinical decisions and report, in a preprint, that general-purpose models produced hallucination-free answers more often than medically specialised ones (median 76.6 against 51.3 per cent) [@kim2025medhallucination], consistent with combining a general open-weight model with grounding and gating.

Grounding lowers but does not eliminate hallucination. Wołk compared twelve RAG pipelines on 250 de-identified cases: hybrid retrieval with reciprocal rank fusion and cross-encoder reranking retrieved best, yet the best variant still hallucinated in 5.8 per cent of answers, and the author recommends confidence thresholds, verification and on-premises deployment without defining when to answer, abstain or escalate [@wolk2025ragcds]. Asgari et al. measured 1.47 per cent hallucinated and 3.45 per cent omitted sentences across 12,999 clinician-annotated sentences of LLM-generated notes, although the full transcript was available [@asgari2025creola]. A preprint systematic review comparing RAG with fine-tuning recommends hybrid approaches [@jubair2026ragft]. A grounded system still needs an explicit decision about whether its evidence suffices before it speaks.

## Abstention, Selective Prediction and Self-Correcting Generation

Selective classification calibrates a confidence threshold so that the error rate on accepted inputs stays below a target [@geifman2017selective]; rejecting more lowers risk but reduces coverage, so the two must be reported together. Conformal prediction gives finite-sample, marginal coverage guarantees under exchangeability [@angelopoulos2023conformal], but extending it to generation needs repeated sampling [@quach2024conformal], which is costly on local hardware. A preprint reports that calibration of self-assessed confidence weakens out of distribution and for smaller models [@kadavath2022know], so MedBridge's gates reason over explicit inputs (the patient snapshot and the retrieved passages) rather than over self-assessed confidence, and its V1 variant calibrates evidence thresholds on the development split without claiming formal guarantees. A survey of abstention notes that its evaluation, including over-abstention, remains immature [@wen2025abstention]; Chapter 4 therefore reports both appropriate abstention and over-abstention.

Chen et al. treat RAG as risk control, refusing low-confidence questions whose confidence is estimated by counterfactually varying the retrieved passages, and lower risk on abstention-aware open-domain benchmarks [@chen2024controlling]. This is the closest prior work to ABSTAIN, but it targets factoid questions and has no mechanism for requesting missing context or for escalation. Self-RAG trains a model to emit reflection tokens that decide whether to retrieve and whether passages and segments are relevant and supported [@asai2024selfrag]; it is the closest prior work to gating, but it needs a specially trained generator, its decisions are learned tokens rather than auditable decisions, and it has no abstain or escalate action. Corrective RAG (CRAG), a preprint, scores retrieved documents and refines them or falls back to web search [@yan2024crag]; its post-retrieval decision is the nearest analogue to the Evidence Gate, but it always answers, uses the open web and ignores patient context.

Post-hoc methods let the model answer first. Chain-of-Verification drafts, verifies with independent questions and revises [@dhuliawala2024cove]; RARR retrieves evidence for each claim, edits unsupported content and attaches an attribution report [@gao2023rarr]; SelfCheckGPT flags sentences that are inconsistent across samples [@manakul2023selfcheckgpt]; and FActScore reports the fraction of atomic facts supported by a trusted source [@min2023factscore], the basis of this project's claim-level evaluation. As safety mechanisms these methods act only after an unsafe draft exists, need further generation that can introduce new errors, and cost several calls per query. MedBridge decides *before* generation and adds post-generation verification (V2) only as a third gate.

## Medical Language Models, Medical RAG and Benchmarks

Med-PaLM 2 reached up to 86.5 per cent on MedQA, was preferred to physician answers on most axes and was tested on adversarial questions [@singhal2025medpalm2], but it is a proprietary cloud model evaluated mainly on examination-style questions. Reviews weigh efficiency against inaccuracy, fabrication, misuse and patient-data privacy [@thirunavukarasu2023llmmedicine; @tian2024chatgptbiomed]: fluency is not evidence of safety.

Almanac connects an LLM to curated medical resources and outperformed general chatbots on factuality and adversarial safety in a clinician evaluation of 314 questions [@zakka2024almanac], but it depends on a cloud LLM and has no context check or abstain and escalate decision. MedCite improves citation precision and recall in medical answers [@wang2025medcite], while SourceCheckup found that 50 to 90 per cent of responses from seven LLMs were not fully supported by their own citations [@wu2025sourcecheckup]; neither decides whether to answer. MIRAGE and MedRAG compare 41 corpus, retriever and model combinations on 7,663 multiple-choice questions, with gains of up to 18 per cent from retrieval [@xiong2024medrag], but every item must be answered. Med-HALT probes false confidence and fake questions in closed-book settings [@pal2023medhalt], and RABBITS shows accuracy drops of about 1 to 10 per cent when brand and generic drug names are swapped [@gallifant2024rabbits], which motivates BM25 retrieval, a brand-to-generic table with Indian brands and a RABBITS-style test in MedBridge. None of these benchmarks measures answerability, abstention or patient context in dialogue, which MedBridge-HalluBench is designed to test.

For local deployment, Qwen3 offers dense and mixture-of-experts models with switchable "thinking" [@yang2025qwen3] and Gemma 3 lightweight open models with longer context [@gemmateam2025gemma3]; the application model, `qwen3.5:4b`, belongs to the later Qwen3.5 generation, which the Qwen3 report does not cover, and the judge, `gemma3:12b`, to Gemma 3. Both reports are written by the vendors, so the models' behaviour in cardiology is established experimentally in Chapter 4. Ollama serves models locally [@ollama2026] and Qdrant stores dense and sparse vectors [@qdrant2026]. Because Ollama also offers cloud-hosted models, MedBridge's local-only mode accepts only an Ollama endpoint on a loopback address and refuses cloud-offloaded model tags.

## Evaluating Hallucination: Judges, Entailment and Statistics

A strong LLM judge agrees with human preferences about as well as humans agree with each other but shows position, verbosity and self-enhancement biases [@zheng2023judging], and LLMs rate their own outputs more favourably [@panickssery2024llm]. Hence the precautions adopted here: the LLM judge comes from a different model family, labels individual claims against evidence under a strict rubric, and is paired with an entailment judge and a human annotation subset. Natural language inference (NLI) models, usually trained on MultiNLI [@williams2018mnli] and often on a DeBERTaV3 backbone [@he2023debertav3], work best for consistency checking when texts are split into sentences and pairwise scores aggregated [@laban2022summac]. Combined with atomic claims [@min2023factscore], this defines the NLI judge; because MultiNLI contains no clinical text, its threshold is calibrated on the development split.

Because every condition answers the same queries, comparisons are paired: the exact McNemar test uses the discordant pairs [@mcnemar1947note], Cochran's Q extends the comparison to several conditions [@cochran1950comparison] and Holm's procedure controls the family-wise error rate [@holm1979simple]. All proportions take Wilson intervals, which stay well behaved near zero or one [@wilson1927probable], and the unsupported-claim ratio and retrieval scores take bootstrap intervals [@efron1993bootstrap]. Judge agreement is measured with Cohen's kappa [@cohen1960coefficient], read against the conventional bands of Landis and Koch [@landis1977measurement] and reported with raw agreement, because kappa is low when one label is rare.

## Retrieval Components

BM25 scores documents by saturated term frequency with length normalisation [@robertson2009bm25]; it matches drug names and numbers exactly but cannot link "high blood pressure" to "hypertension". Dense Passage Retrieval beat BM25 by 9 to 19 points in top-20 accuracy on open-domain question answering [@karpukhin2020dpr], and the BGE family provides compact English encoders [@xiao2024cpack]. Hybrid pipelines with fusion did best on clinical cases [@wolk2025ragcds]. Reciprocal rank fusion (RRF) sums 1/(k + rank) over the ranked lists, needs no score calibration and is commonly used with k = 60 [@cormack2009rrf], which suits cosine and BM25 scores on different scales. A cross-encoder reads query and passage together; in a preprint, a BERT cross-encoder that reranked BM25 candidates topped the MS MARCO passage leaderboard [@nogueira2019passage]; distilled MiniLM models keep most of a teacher's accuracy with about half the computation [@wang2020minilm], making reranking affordable locally. Whether these general-domain results hold on cardiology text is tested in Chapter 4.

## Security, Privacy and Regulation

A model cannot reliably tell instructions from the data it processes. *Indirect* prompt injection plants instructions in content that an application later retrieves [@greshake2023notwhat], so retrieved passages are untrusted too; a benchmark of five attacks against ten defences found none sufficient on its own [@liu2024formalizing]; and a review of 128 studies reports that architectural defences block up to about 95 per cent of known patterns while cautioning that prompt-level defences carry no formal guarantees [@geng2026promptinjection]. The OWASP Top 10 for LLM Applications (2025 edition) consolidates these concerns into community guidance [@owasp2025llmtop10]. Table 2.1 maps its relevant risks to MedBridge's layered controls.

Table: OWASP LLM Top 10 (2025) risks and MedBridge controls
| Risk (2025 identifier) | MedBridge control |
|---|---|
| LLM01 Prompt Injection | Patient text in untrusted-input XML blocks with tag neutralisation; deterministic emergency path and templates; adversarial test suite |
| LLM02 Sensitive Information Disclosure | Redaction before any model call and storage; local-only inference; encryption at rest; log scrubbing |
| LLM05 Improper Output Handling | Escape-first Markdown rendering; strict Content Security Policy |
| LLM06 Excessive Agency | Models have no tools; only the orchestrator accesses the database and vector store |
| LLM08 Vector and Embedding Weaknesses | Curated corpus pinned by checksums; write access only through offline ingestion |
| LLM09 Misinformation | Evidence Gate; citation validation and downgrade; ABSTAIN and GENERALIZE outcomes |
| LLM10 Unbounded Consumption | Rate limiting; 2,000-character message limit; request size limit |

India's DPDP Act, 2023 requires reasonable security safeguards (Section 8(5)), erasure when consent is withdrawn or the purpose is served (Section 8(7)), and recognises a medical emergency as a legitimate use (Section 7(f)) [@dpdp2023india]. The U.S. HIPAA Privacy Rule does not bind an Indian project but serves as a design benchmark [@hhs_hipaa_privacy_rule]; its Safe Harbor method lists eighteen identifier categories [@hhs2012deidentification], which informed the categories of MedBridge's redactor, extended with Aadhaar, PAN and PIN codes. MedBridge uses a light rule-based redactor that leaves clinical values untouched rather than a statistical entity detector. Event sourcing records every change as an appended event that can be replayed and audited [@fowler2005eventsourcing], but does not say how personal data can be erased from an immutable log. Cryptographic erase, which destroys the encryption key [@nist2025sp80088r2], together with key-management guidance [@barker2020sp80057pt1r5], resolves this per conversation (crypto-shredding). WHO guidance on AI for health emphasises safety, transparency and human oversight [@who2021ethicsai]; MedBridge is not a medical device, but its audit logging, transparency panel and conservative actions follow this guidance.

## Patent Review

A keyword search of Google Patents (clinical decision support with LLMs, answerability and abstention, hallucination detection, RAG verification) identified six granted patents related to parts of the design, each checked against its public record (Table 2.3). The comparison positions the technical contribution and is not a legal opinion on novelty or freedom to operate. The closest is US 12,142,371 B1 (Hippocratic AI) [@shah2024hippocraticconstellation], in which control logic, implemented in the described embodiments as parallel LLMs, evaluates a patient conversation and can hand a "kickout" answer to a human; MedBridge's emergency path is instead rule-based and runs before any generation. The Intuit disambiguation patent [@zhang2024intuitdisambiguity] is the nearest analogue to SOFT-ASK, but it is triggered by linguistic ambiguity rather than by missing clinical facts, and has no cap on clarification or evidence gate.

The individual ingredients exist in prior work: evidence evaluation in CRAG and Self-RAG, refusal in risk-controlled RAG and the IBM patent, citation checking in MedCite and RARR, hand-off to humans in the Hippocratic AI patent and local deployment as a recommendation in clinical RAG studies. No approach combines a pre-generation check of *patient context* with a separate check of *evidence*, deterministic emergency handling, local inference and citation validation, and none evaluates such gating on hallucination through a paired ablation with two different judges (a cross-family LLM judge and an NLI judge).

The research gap can therefore be stated precisely. Within the literature reviewed, no system makes a **pre-generation, two-part answerability decision** that separates *"I do not know enough about you"* (insufficient patient context, decided before retrieval) from *"I do not have enough evidence"* (insufficient guideline support, decided after retrieval but before generation), routes the outcome to a fixed set of graded actions with deterministic templates for refusal and emergency referral, and places a deterministic emergency check in front of both decisions. Nor has any study measured whether such gating reduces hallucination and unsafe answers compared with retrieval alone and with prompt-only safety instructions, through a paired ablation with a cross-family LLM judge and an NLI judge on fully local open-weight models. MedBridge addresses this gap with the Dual Answerability Gate (Chapter 3) and the MedBridge-HalluBench study (Chapter 4).
