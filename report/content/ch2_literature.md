# Literature and Patent Review

This chapter reviews the work on which MedBridge builds and against which it must be judged. It begins with the clinical guidance that defines what a correct answer looks like, then moves through retrieval-augmented generation and hallucination, abstention and self-correcting generation, medical language models and benchmarks, methods for evaluating hallucination, the retrieval components used in the system, prompt-injection security, and privacy regulation. It then revisits the papers cited in the Review-1 presentation, reviews related patents, compares existing approaches with MedBridge, summarises the limitations of existing work and states the research gap that this project addresses.

## Review Methodology

The review was organised around the questions that the design of MedBridge raises: what clinical sources define safe guidance, how grounded generation fails, how a system can decide not to answer, how medical LLMs have been built and evaluated, how hallucination can be measured reliably, which retrieval components suit a small local deployment, and which security and privacy obligations apply. Table 2.1 lists the themes and the sections in which they are discussed.

Sources were selected with a preference for peer-reviewed journal and conference papers, official guideline documents and primary legal or standards texts. Foundational papers were retained for retrieval methods and statistics, while work on LLMs was drawn mainly from 2022 onwards. Preprints are used only where no peer-reviewed version exists and are identified as such in the text. Every bibliographic record was checked against the corresponding publisher, proceedings or registry entry, such as the DOI registry, the ACL Anthology, PubMed records and official organisational pages, and every patent was checked against its public patent record. The three earlier project documents (the Review-1 and Review-3 presentations and the design specification) were used to recover the literature claims made at earlier reviews, which are matched to specific publications in Section 2.10.

## Clinical Background: Hypertension Guidance and Its Variability

A guideline-grounded assistant inherits both the strengths and the inconsistencies of its sources, so the clinical literature is reviewed first. The 2025 AHA/ACC multisociety guideline is the current United States reference for BP measurement, classification, risk-based initiation of treatment, targets and severe hypertension; it replaces the 2017 ACC/AHA guideline and has received several published corrections [@jones2025aha]. The 2017 guideline, which lowered the diagnostic threshold to 130/80 mmHg, remains the classic source for separating a hypertensive emergency, in which severely raised BP (typically above 180/120 mmHg) comes with new or worsening target-organ damage, from hypertensive urgency without such damage [@whelton2018acc], a distinction also drawn by a clinical review of acute severe hypertension [@peixoto2019acute].

European guidance does not speak with one voice. The 2024 ESC guideline adds an elevated-BP category of 120 to 139 / 70 to 89 mmHg and recommends a treated systolic target of 120 to 129 mmHg where tolerated [@mcevoy2024esc], whereas the 2023 ESH guideline retains the classic grades of hypertension [@mancia2023esh]. The 2020 ISH guideline separates "essential" care for low-resource settings from "optimal" care [@unger2020ish], and the WHO guideline on pharmacological treatment covers thresholds, targets, first-line classes, combination therapy, follow-up and task sharing, but excludes pregnancy [@who2021pharm]. NICE NG136 lists criteria for same-day specialist referral, including a clinic BP of 180/120 mmHg or higher with retinal haemorrhage, papilloedema or life-threatening symptoms such as new confusion, chest pain or signs of heart failure [@nice2019ng136]. Co-existing conditions change the correct answer further: KDIGO recommends a systolic target below 120 mmHg in CKD with standardised measurement [@kdigo2021bp], the American Diabetes Association revises its cardiovascular recommendations every year [@ada2026cvd], and the heart failure [@heidenreich2022hf], atrial fibrillation [@joglar2024af], chronic coronary disease [@virani2023ccd] and primary prevention [@arnett2019primary] guidelines embed BP advice within condition-specific care that assumes clinician oversight of medicines.

Five design implications follow, summarised in Table 2.2. Answers must cite the specific source they rely on, because sources differ; many questions cannot be answered safely without facts such as kidney disease, diabetes or pregnancy, which motivates a context check; emergency criteria are explicit enough to be encoded as deterministic rules; clinician-oriented text must be rephrased for patients without losing its grounding; and because guidelines are revised, the knowledge base must record each document's edition. Chapter 3 describes which documents could be included in the knowledge base under their licence terms.

## Retrieval-Augmented Generation and Hallucination

### Retrieval-Augmented Generation

Retrieval-augmented generation, introduced by Lewis et al., couples a pretrained sequence-to-sequence generator with a dense retriever over an external document collection, treating the retrieved passages as a latent variable and fine-tuning both components together [@lewis2020rag]. The approach improved open-domain question answering and made generations more factual than those of purely parametric models, and it has the practical advantage that knowledge can be updated by changing the document collection rather than retraining the model. Its limitation for a safety-critical setting is structural: retrieval is always performed and an answer is always produced, so the system has no explicit way to decline when the retrieved evidence is weak.

A broad survey organises later work into *naive* retrieve-then-read pipelines, *advanced* pipelines that add query rewriting and reranking, and *modular* designs that compose routers, retrievers, rerankers and verifiers [@gao2023ragsurvey]. In these terms MedBridge is a modular RAG system that adds query expansion and drug-name normalisation before retrieval, fuses two retrievers with reranking, and inserts decision modules (the two gates) before and after retrieval. The survey is a general-domain preprint without empirical comparison, so it serves here as a map of the field rather than as evidence.

### Taxonomies of Hallucination

The survey by Ji et al. provides the basic vocabulary: *intrinsic* hallucinations contradict the source, whereas *extrinsic* hallucinations add content that the source can neither confirm nor refute [@ji2023survey]. Because that survey largely predates instruction-tuned LLMs, the later survey by Huang et al. supplies the LLM-specific view, dividing errors into *factuality* errors (conflicts with world knowledge) and *faithfulness* errors (departures from the instructions or supplied context) and discussing failure modes that remain in retrieval-augmented systems [@huang2025survey]. For MedBridge the consequence is that retrieval failure and unfaithful generation are separate risks: the first is addressed before generation by the Evidence Gate, the second by restricting generation to retrieved passages, validating citations and, in the V2 extension, verifying claims after generation.

In medicine, Kim et al. define a medical hallucination as output that is unsupported or incorrect in a way that could alter clinical decisions, and benchmark eleven foundation models on seven hallucination tasks [@kim2025medhallucination]. General-purpose models produced hallucination-free answers more often than medically specialised ones (a median of 76.6 against 51.3 per cent). The work is a preprint and not specific to cardiology, but the finding is consistent with MedBridge's choice of a general open-weight model combined with grounding and gating rather than a medically fine-tuned model.

### Hallucination That Persists Under Grounding

Several recent studies show that grounding lowers but does not eliminate hallucination. Wołk compared twelve types of RAG pipeline on 250 de-identified patient cases and found that hybrid retrieval with reciprocal rank fusion and cross-encoder reranking retrieved best, yet even the best variant still hallucinated in 5.8 per cent of answers [@wolk2025ragcds]. The author therefore recommends retrieval-confidence thresholds, explicit verification and on-premises deployment with encryption and audit trails, but does not define a rule for when a system should answer, abstain or escalate.

Asgari et al. proposed an error taxonomy, a clinical-safety harm assessment and a review interface (CREOLA) for LLM-generated clinical notes [@asgari2025creola]. Across 18 configurations and 12,999 clinician-annotated sentences they measured a hallucination rate of 1.47 per cent and an omission rate of 3.45 per cent even though the full consultation transcript was available, showing that access to the source does not guarantee faithfulness and providing a precedent for sentence-level, clinician-defined error labels.

A systematic review posted as a preprint compares RAG with fine-tuning for medical LLMs and, according to its indexed abstract, recommends hybrid approaches [@jubair2026ragft]; it is the closest, but uncertain, match to one Review-1 row (Section 2.10). Together with the citation audits in Section 2.5, these studies motivate the central premise of this project: a grounded system still needs an explicit decision about whether its evidence is sufficient before it speaks.

## Abstention, Selective Prediction and Self-Correcting Generation

### Selective Prediction and Calibrated Abstention

The idea that a model may decline goes back to classification with a reject option. Geifman and El-Yaniv calibrate a confidence threshold so that, with high probability, the error rate on accepted inputs stays below a chosen target [@geifman2017selective]. This frames the trade-off evaluated in this report: rejecting more inputs lowers risk on answered inputs but reduces coverage, so the two must be reported together as a risk-coverage curve. Conformal prediction provides finite-sample coverage guarantees for any model under exchangeability [@angelopoulos2023conformal], and conformal language modelling extends such guarantees to generation through calibrated sampling and rejection rules [@quach2024conformal]. These guarantees are marginal rather than per query, assume calibration data similar to deployment data and, for generation, need repeated sampling that is costly on local hardware. MedBridge therefore adopts calibration on held-out data (its V1 extension calibrates evidence thresholds on the development split) without claiming formal guarantees.

Whether models know what they know is a related question. Large models are reasonably well calibrated on suitably formatted multiple-choice questions, but calibration weakens out of distribution and for smaller models [@kadavath2022know], so MedBridge's gates reason over explicit inputs, the structured patient snapshot and the retrieved passages, rather than over self-assessed confidence alone. Wen et al. survey abstention from the perspectives of the query (is it answerable or safe), the model (is it confident) and human values (should it be answered), and note that the evaluation of abstention, including over-abstention, remains immature [@wen2025abstention]. Following that observation, Chapter 4 reports both appropriate abstention on unanswerable questions and over-abstention on answerable ones.

### Risk-Controlled Retrieval-Augmented Generation

Chen et al. frame RAG as a risk-control problem in which low-confidence questions should be refused [@chen2024controlling]. They estimate confidence by counterfactually varying the quality of the retrieved passages and the way they are used, and on abstention-aware versions of TriviaQA and Natural Questions their method lowers risk and increases carefulness compared with baselines. This is the closest prior work to MedBridge's ABSTAIN outcome, but it addresses open-domain factoid questions and has no mechanism for requesting missing patient context or for escalation. C-RAG, on the theoretical side, gives a conformal upper bound on the generation risk of retrieval-augmented models [@kang2024crag]; it should not be confused with Corrective RAG (CRAG), discussed next.

### Self-Reflective and Corrective RAG

Self-RAG fine-tunes a single language model to emit special reflection tokens that decide whether to retrieve, judge whether each retrieved passage is relevant and judge whether each generated segment is supported [@asai2024selfrag]. At inference these tokens steer retrieval and the selection of segments, improving factuality and citation accuracy. Self-RAG is the closest prior work to MedBridge's gating idea because it reasons explicitly about retrieval and support, but it differs in three ways that matter here: it requires a specially trained generator, its decisions are learned tokens rather than auditable decisions with recorded rationales, and it has no explicit abstain or escalate action.

Corrective RAG (CRAG) adds a lightweight retrieval evaluator that scores the retrieved documents and, depending on a confidence band, refines them, discards them in favour of web search, or combines both, with a decompose-then-recompose filter on the documents [@yan2024crag]. CRAG's post-retrieval decision is the closest analogue to MedBridge's Evidence Gate. However, CRAG always produces an answer, falls back to the open web, which an offline clinical assistant must not do, and has no notion of patient context. It is also available only as a preprint.

### Post-Hoc Verification, Attribution and Factuality Scoring

A different family of methods lets the model answer first and checks the answer afterwards. Chain-of-Verification (CoVe) drafts an answer, plans verification questions, answers them independently and revises the draft, reducing hallucinated facts in list-style and closed-book question answering and in biographies [@dhuliawala2024cove]; since it uses only the model's own knowledge, shared misconceptions survive it. RARR retrieves evidence for the claims in any output, edits unsupported content and attaches an attribution report [@gao2023rarr], but relies on web search and repairs text only after it exists. SelfCheckGPT flags sentences that are inconsistent across several sampled responses, without external knowledge [@manakul2023selfcheckgpt], at the cost of many generations and with no defence against consistently repeated errors. FActScore decomposes long-form text into atomic facts and reports the fraction supported by a trusted source, with an automatic estimator that tracks human judgement [@min2023factscore]; it measures precision rather than completeness and depends on the coverage of the reference corpus.

FActScore underpins the claim-level evaluation used in this project. As safety mechanisms, however, post-hoc methods share a weakness noted early in the MedBridge design specification: once an unsafe draft exists, correcting it needs further generation, which can introduce new errors, at a cost paid on every query. MedBridge instead decides *before* generation whether to answer at all; its V2 extension adds a post-generation grounding verifier as a third gate, strictly in addition to the two pre-generation gates.

## Medical Large Language Models and Medical RAG

### Medical Foundation Models

Med-PaLM was evaluated on the MultiMedQA benchmark with a clinician rating framework covering factuality, reasoning, possible harm and bias, and despite state-of-the-art multiple-choice accuracy it still fell short of clinicians in human review [@singhal2023llmclinical]. Med-PaLM 2 added medical fine-tuning, ensemble refinement and chain-of-retrieval, reported up to 86.5 per cent on MedQA, was preferred to physician answers on eight of nine axes and was tested on adversarial question sets [@singhal2025medpalm2]. Both are proprietary cloud models evaluated mostly on examination-style questions, so they say little about a small local model with deterministic triage and abstention.

Reviews of LLMs in medicine and biomedicine weigh efficiency gains against inaccuracy, fabricated content, misuse and patient-data privacy [@thirunavukarasu2023llmmedicine; @tian2024chatgptbiomed]. Studies of deployed chat models make these concerns concrete: health professionals preferred chatbot replies to physician replies on quality and empathy rather than verified correctness [@ayers2023physicianchatbot], and four commercial models reproduced debunked race-based medical claims with inconsistent answers across runs [@omiye2023racebased]. Fluency and perceived helpfulness are therefore not evidence of safety, and answers should be grounded in curated evidence rather than in whatever a model absorbed during pretraining.

### Retrieval-Augmented Clinical Systems and Citation

Almanac connects an LLM to curated medical resources and, in an evaluation by clinicians on 314 questions across nine specialties, outperformed ChatGPT-4, Bing and Bard on factuality, completeness, user preference and adversarial safety [@zakka2024almanac]. It is the closest clinical RAG system to MedBridge, but it depends on a cloud LLM and has neither a pre-retrieval context check nor a calibrated abstain or escalate decision.

Citations let a reader check a grounded answer, yet they are often unreliable. MedCite introduces a multi-pass retrieval-and-citation method that improves citation precision and recall in medical answers, with an automatic citation evaluation that agrees well with experts [@wang2025medcite]. SourceCheckup audited about 58,000 statement-source pairs from seven LLMs and found that 50 to 90 per cent of responses were not fully supported by their own citations, with about 30 per cent of statements unsupported even for a model using web search [@wu2025sourcecheckup]. Neither work decides whether to answer at all. MedBridge validates every citation marker against the passages retrieved for that turn, removes markers that match no passage and downgrades an answer left with no valid citation.

### Medical Hallucination and Robustness Benchmarks

Table 2.3 summarises the main medical evaluation benchmarks. MIRAGE (7,663 questions) and the MedRAG toolkit compare 41 combinations of corpora, retrievers and LLMs; retrieval improved six LLMs by up to 18 per cent over chain-of-thought prompting, and the authors also report log-linear gains with the number of snippets and a "lost-in-the-middle" effect [@xiong2024medrag]. Every item, however, is a multiple-choice question that must be answered, leaving answerability, abstention and dialogue out of scope. Med-HALT probes false confidence, fake questions and the recall of references in closed-book examination settings [@pal2023medhalt]; its fake-question tests resemble MedBridge's unanswerable category but do not measure grounding or refusal in open dialogue.

RABBITS applies physician-annotated brand-to-generic drug-name swaps to two medical question-answering benchmarks and finds accuracy drops of about 1 to 10 per cent in open and commercial LLMs [@gallifant2024rabbits]. Because patients name antihypertensives by many brands, this motivates three elements of MedBridge: BM25 retrieval alongside dense retrieval, a brand-to-generic table that includes Indian brands, and a RABBITS-style robustness evaluation.

### Open-Weight Models for Local Deployment

Running the assistant on a laptop requires capable open-weight models and a local serving layer. Qwen3 offers open dense and mixture-of-experts models that switch between a step-by-step "thinking" mode and a faster direct mode [@yang2025qwen3], and Gemma 3 offers lightweight open models with longer context and better multilingual and instruction-following ability [@gemmateam2025gemma3]. Both are vendor reports on general benchmarks, so their behaviour in cardiology must be established experimentally; the model-selection experiment in Chapter 4 compares candidates on structured-output validity, routing accuracy and latency. Drawing the evaluation judge from a different model family also limits the self-preference bias discussed in Section 2.6. Ollama serves the models locally behind an HTTP interface [@ollama2026], and Qdrant, an open-source vector search engine supporting dense and sparse vectors, stores the indexed passages [@qdrant2026]. Because Ollama now also offers cloud-hosted models, MedBridge's local-only mode explicitly restricts inference to local hosts.

## Evaluating Hallucination: Judges, Entailment and Statistics

### LLM-as-a-Judge and Its Biases

Human evaluation of every response is too expensive for a study with several conditions and hundreds of queries, so automatic judges are needed. Zheng et al. showed that a strong LLM judge agrees with human preferences about as well as humans agree with each other, while documenting position, verbosity and self-enhancement biases [@zheng2023judging], and Panickssery et al. showed that LLMs recognise their own outputs and rate them more favourably than human annotators do [@panickssery2024llm]. Both findings come from general tasks rather than clinical fact-checking, but they motivate three precautions adopted here: the LLM judge comes from a different model family from the application model, it labels individual claims against evidence under a strict rubric instead of comparing answers, and it is paired with an independent entailment judge and a human annotation subset.

### Entailment-Based Factual Consistency

Natural language inference (NLI) models classify whether a premise entails, contradicts or is neutral towards a hypothesis. They are usually trained on MultiNLI, about 433,000 sentence pairs across ten genres [@williams2018mnli], often on a DeBERTaV3 backbone [@he2023debertav3]. SummaC showed that NLI-based consistency checks work far better when both texts are split into sentences and the pairwise scores aggregated, rather than applying sentence-pair models to whole documents [@laban2022summac]. Combined with the atomic-claim decomposition of FActScore [@min2023factscore], these results define the NLI judge used in this work: each claim is scored against the best-matching evidence passages and counted as supported when the entailment score exceeds a threshold. Since MultiNLI contains no clinical text, that threshold is calibrated on the development split against the LLM judge rather than taken from the literature.

### Statistical Methods for Paired Comparisons

Because every condition in the ablation answers the same queries, the comparisons are paired. McNemar's test compares two paired binary outcomes using only the discordant pairs, in its exact binomial form when such pairs are few [@mcnemar1947note]; Cochran's Q extends the comparison to more than two matched conditions [@cochran1950comparison]; and Holm's step-down procedure controls the family-wise error rate of the pairwise tests with less conservatism than Bonferroni [@holm1979simple]. Proportions that are often close to zero or one are reported with Wilson score intervals [@wilson1927probable], and metrics without closed-form intervals, such as claim ratios and retrieval scores, with bootstrap intervals [@efron1993bootstrap]. Agreement between judges, and between each judge and human labels, is measured with Cohen's kappa [@cohen1960coefficient] and read against the conventional, admittedly arbitrary, bands of Landis and Koch [@landis1977measurement]; because kappa can be low when one label is rare even if raw agreement is high, raw agreement is reported alongside it.

## Retrieval Components

### Lexical, Dense and Learned-Sparse Retrieval

BM25, derived from the probabilistic relevance framework, scores documents by saturated term frequency with length normalisation [@robertson2009bm25]. It matches drug names, abbreviations and numbers precisely but cannot connect paraphrases such as "high blood pressure" and "hypertension". Dense retrieval fills this gap: Dense Passage Retrieval encodes questions and passages separately for nearest-neighbour search and beat a strong BM25 baseline by 9 to 19 points in top-20 accuracy on open-domain question answering [@karpukhin2020dpr]; Sentence-BERT made such bi-encoder embeddings practical [@reimers2019sbert]; and the BGE family released with C-Pack provides compact general-purpose English encoders trained contrastively [@xiao2024cpack]. MedBridge uses the small English BGE model, whose effectiveness on cardiology text is measured in this project's retrieval evaluation. Learned sparse models such as SPLADE add expansion terms to an inverted index and approach dense effectiveness [@formal2021splade], but add a neural model to the sparse path, so plain BM25 was preferred for its low cost.

### Hybrid Retrieval and Rank Fusion

Combining lexical and dense retrieval is well supported. A reproduction of DPR found that the original paper under-reported its BM25 baseline and hence the benefit of dense-sparse hybrids [@ma2022dprhybrid]; MedRAG supports combining retrievers with fusion in medicine [@xiong2024medrag]; and Wołk found hybrid pipelines with reciprocal rank fusion best on clinical cases [@wolk2025ragcds]. Reciprocal rank fusion (RRF) sums 1/(k + rank) over the ranked lists in which a document appears, needs no score calibration or training, outperformed Condorcet fusion and learned combination on TREC collections, and is commonly used with k = 60 [@cormack2009rrf]. Because cosine similarities and BM25 scores lie on different scales, rank-based fusion suits MedBridge, although RRF weights every ranker equally and was tested on general collections, so its value on clinical text is checked in the retrieval evaluation.

### Cross-Encoder Reranking

Bi-encoders compress each passage into one vector and lose fine-grained query-passage interactions. A cross-encoder reads the query and passage together; fine-tuning BERT in this way to rerank BM25 candidates topped the MS MARCO passage leaderboard at the time [@nogueira2019passage]. Each candidate costs one forward pass, so distilled models such as MiniLM, which keep most of a teacher's accuracy with about half the computation [@wang2020minilm], make reranking affordable locally. MedBridge reranks the top 20 fused candidates to the top 5 with a MiniLM cross-encoder trained on MS MARCO, in a worker thread so the web server is not blocked; its contribution is measured in the retrieval evaluation and a reranker ablation in Chapter 4.

## Prompt Injection and Application Security

LLM applications are vulnerable because a model cannot reliably tell instructions apart from the data it processes. Greshake et al. introduced *indirect* prompt injection, in which instructions planted in content that an application later retrieves take effect, and demonstrated it on deployed systems [@greshake2023notwhat]; for RAG this means that retrieved passages, not only user messages, are untrusted. Liu et al. formalised prompt-injection attacks and benchmarked five attacks against ten defences on ten LLMs and seven tasks, concluding that the defences tested were insufficient on their own [@liu2024formalizing]. A review of 128 studies traces the problem to the confusion of instructions with data, reports that input preprocessing detects roughly 60 to 80 per cent of attacks and architectural defences block up to about 95 per cent of known patterns, and cautions that prompt-level defences carry no formal guarantees [@geng2026promptinjection].

The OWASP Top 10 for LLM Applications (2025 edition) consolidates these concerns into an industry risk list with mitigations [@owasp2025llmtop10]. It is community guidance rather than peer-reviewed research, and a later edition has renumbered the risks, so the identifiers below refer to the 2025 edition. Table 2.4 maps the relevant risks to MedBridge's controls. Because no single defence suffices, MedBridge relies on layers: patient text and retrieved evidence are enclosed in dedicated XML blocks with tag neutralisation; the emergency path and the refusal and referral templates involve no model at all; routing decisions are taken by separate gate prompts that apply the same isolation rather than by the generator; the models have no tools or database access; and an adversarial test suite exercises these defences.

| LLM10 Unbounded Consumption | Resource exhaustion | Rate limiting; 2,000-character message limit; request body size limit |

## Privacy, Regulation and Ethics

India's Digital Personal Data Protection Act, 2023 requires data fiduciaries to apply reasonable security safeguards (Section 8(5)) and to erase personal data when consent is withdrawn or the purpose has been served (Section 8(7)), and recognises responding to a medical emergency as a legitimate use (Section 7(f)) [@dpdp2023india]. It does not define health data as a separate sensitive category, so health-specific obligations must be inferred. The U.S. HIPAA Privacy Rule, which protects individually identifiable health information held by covered entities, does not bind an Indian project but serves as a design benchmark [@hhs_hipaa_privacy_rule]. Its Safe Harbor de-identification method removes eighteen categories of identifiers, including names, small geographic units, dates, telephone numbers and medical record numbers [@hhs2012deidentification]; these informed the categories targeted by MedBridge's redactor, which adds Indian identifiers such as Aadhaar and PAN numbers and PIN codes. Safe Harbor is a checklist rather than a risk measure, so free text can still leak identity through quasi-identifiers. Presidio, an open-source framework originally developed by Microsoft, detects personal data with named-entity models, patterns, checksums and context words [@presidio2026]; it is cited as related tooling, since MedBridge implements a lighter, rule-based redactor tailored to clinical text and designed to leave BP values, laboratory results, drug names and doses untouched.

Erasure is complicated by the audit design. Event sourcing records every state change as an appended event, so that state can be rebuilt by replay and the history serves as an audit trail [@fowler2005eventsourcing]; MedBridge stores patient facts and gate decisions this way in append-only tables, but the pattern does not say how personal data can be removed from an immutable log. NIST guidance on media sanitisation includes cryptographic erase, which makes data unrecoverable by destroying its encryption key [@nist2025sp80088r2], and NIST key-management guidance covers how such keys are generated, protected and destroyed [@barker2020sp80057pt1r5]. MedBridge applies this per conversation (crypto-shredding): each session is encrypted with its own key, and erasure destroys the key while the append-only rows remain but become unreadable, which assumes the key was never copied elsewhere.

WHO guidance on AI for health sets out six ethical principles, including protecting human autonomy and ensuring safety, transparency and accountability [@who2021ethicsai]; its emphasis on human oversight supports MedBridge's choice to abstain or escalate rather than guess and to expose the evidence behind every answer. The EU Artificial Intelligence Act treats AI that is, or is a safety component of, a medical device requiring third-party conformity assessment as high-risk, with obligations for risk management, logging, transparency and human oversight [@euaiact2024]. MedBridge is neither a medical device nor placed on the EU market; it is an educational decision-support prototype whose audit logging, transparency panel and conservative actions nonetheless follow the spirit of these requirements.

## Patent Review

A keyword search of granted and published patents on Google Patents, covering clinical decision support with LLMs, answerability and abstention, hallucination detection and RAG verification, identified six granted patents that relate to parts of the MedBridge design; each was checked against its public patent record. They are cited by patent number, with titles given in Table 2.6 and full records in the reference list; the comparisons are intended for positioning the technical contribution and are not a legal opinion on novelty or freedom to operate. Table 2.6 summarises them.

The closest patent to MedBridge as a whole is US 12,142,371 B1, granted in 2024 to Healthgpt Inc. (doing business as Hippocratic AI) [@shah2024hippocraticconstellation]. In its multi-turn patient conversations a primary LLM talks with the user while control logic, implemented in the described embodiments as separate LLMs running in parallel, evaluates earlier turns, inserts follow-up questions and classifies answers; an answer in a "kickout" category can end the conversation or transfer it to a human professional. MedBridge's emergency path, by contrast, is rule-based and runs before any generation, and its two answerability gates are not claimed. US 12,243,646 B1 (Glass Health, 2025) [@paul2025glasshealthrag] claims clinician-facing decision support in which retrieval over a physician-validated guideline library feeds an LLM that writes differentials and plans, with no claim to refusal or escalation. US 9,740,769 B2 (IBM, 2017) [@bhagwat2017ibmnoanswer] is early prior art for returning a ranked "no answer" candidate when evidence is insufficient, which MedBridge extends to four graded evidence outcomes preceded by a separate context gate.

Two patents verify or detect hallucination. US 12,505,299 B2 (Casetext, 2025) [@delevie2025casetextverification] has an LLM check each generated portion against the passages it cites, a post-generation design for legal research, while US 12,387,049 B2 (Intuit, 2025) [@zhang2025intuithallucination] detects hallucination without references by measuring consistency across rewritten prompts and two LLMs. MedBridge instead decides before generation, validates citation markers deterministically and uses claim-level verification only as an optional added gate and in offline evaluation. Finally, US 12,038,918 B1 (Intuit, 2024) [@zhang2024intuitdisambiguity] has an LLM detect an ambiguous query and ask a clarifying question, the nearest analogue to SOFT-ASK; its trigger, however, is linguistic ambiguity, whereas MedBridge's Context Gate asks whether patient facts that would change a safe clinical answer are missing, is capped at two consecutive requests and works together with an evidence gate and an emergency path.

## Limitations of Existing Work

The review identifies the following limitations of existing work with respect to a safe, patient-facing cardiovascular assistant:

1. **Answer-by-default designs.** Standard RAG and most medical RAG systems produce an answer for every query, and their benchmarks, which are mostly multiple-choice, require one [@lewis2020rag; @xiong2024medrag]. Residual hallucination remains even with good retrieval [@wolk2025ragcds; @asgari2025creola].
2. **Evidence-only answerability.** Adaptive and corrective RAG methods judge the retrieved evidence [@asai2024selfrag; @yan2024crag] but do not ask whether the patient context is sufficient to personalise it, which is the source of unsafe personalisation.
3. **Repair after the fact.** Post-hoc verification and attribution [@dhuliawala2024cove; @gao2023rarr; @manakul2023selfcheckgpt] act only after a draft exists, add several model calls per query and cannot prevent an unsafe draft from being produced.
4. **Learned or opaque decisions.** Reflection tokens and similar learned mechanisms require custom training and do not produce auditable rationales [@asai2024selfrag], while confidence-based abstention relies on calibration that weakens for small models [@kadavath2022know] and requires domain-specific calibration data [@wen2025abstention; @angelopoulos2023conformal].
5. **No deterministic emergency handling.** None of the reviewed RAG methods provides a rule-based emergency route that bypasses the model; the patented hand-off relies, in its described embodiments, on LLM-based control logic.
6. **Cloud dependence.** The strongest medical systems run on proprietary cloud models [@singhal2025medpalm2; @zakka2024almanac], and several general methods rely on web search, which conflicts with privacy obligations for health data [@dpdp2023india].
7. **Narrow evaluation.** Medical benchmarks focus on multiple-choice accuracy or closed-book tests [@xiong2024medrag; @pal2023medhalt; @gallifant2024rabbits], citation audits do not test gating [@wu2025sourcecheckup; @wang2025medcite], and automatic evaluation with a single LLM judge is exposed to known biases [@zheng2023judging; @panickssery2024llm].
8. **Security and privacy treated separately.** Prompt-injection defences are studied mostly outside clinical RAG pipelines [@liu2024formalizing; @geng2026promptinjection], and the event-sourcing pattern behind tamper-evident audit trails does not itself address the right to erasure [@fowler2005eventsourcing].

## Identified Research Gap

From these limitations the research gap addressed by this project can be stated precisely. Within the literature reviewed, no system makes a **pre-generation, two-part answerability decision** that separates *"I do not know enough about you"* (insufficient patient context, decided before retrieval) from *"I do not have enough evidence"* (insufficient guideline support, decided after retrieval but before generation), routes the outcome to a fixed set of graded actions with deterministic templates for refusal and emergency referral, and places a deterministic emergency check in front of both decisions. Nor has any of the reviewed studies measured whether such gating actually reduces hallucination and unsafe answers compared with retrieval alone and with prompt-only safety instructions, using a paired ablation with independent judges, on fully local open-weight models.

MedBridge addresses this gap with the Dual Answerability Gate, described in Chapter 3, and evaluates it with the MedBridge-HalluBench study reported in Chapter 4. ## Summary

This chapter reviewed the clinical, technical and regulatory literature relevant to MedBridge. Clinical guidelines show that correct hypertension advice depends on patient context and differs between issuing bodies. Retrieval reduces but does not remove hallucination, and existing adaptive, corrective and post-hoc methods either judge only the evidence or act after an answer exists. Medical LLMs and benchmarks emphasise examination-style accuracy and cloud models, while the literature on judges and statistics supplies tools for a paired evaluation; retrieval research supports hybrid lexical and dense retrieval with fusion and reranking; and security and privacy work calls for layered defences, local processing, redaction and cryptographic erasure. The comparison with existing approaches and patents leads to the gap stated above, a pre-generation dual answerability decision that separates insufficient patient context from insufficient evidence, which Chapter 3 now addresses.
